\begin{abstract}
\noindent Claims about reduced-signal medical imaging --- ``50\% dose reduction in CT,'' ``4$\times$ acceleration in MRI,'' ``25\%-activity PET'' --- are routinely reported as single scalar percentages without specifying the diagnostic task, the patient subpopulation, the equivalence margin, or the statistical confidence over which the claim is meant to hold. The resulting literature is neither comparable across studies nor actionable for clinical decision-making, and the gap is widening as reconstruction methods proliferate.
\par
We introduce \textbf{signal-equivalence}, a probabilistic framework that converts every such claim into a $5$-tuple credential $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha, \Pi)$: a reconstruction method is signal-equivalent to a named reference method at signal-reduction ratio $r$, task $T$, equivalence margin $\varepsilon$, and significance level $\alpha$ over patient subpopulation $\Pi$ if and only if its expected task performance on reduced-signal scans is within $\varepsilon$ of the reference method's performance on full-signal scans, with confidence at least $1-\alpha$. The framework is modality-general by construction --- a single definition covers CT tube-current reduction, MRI $k$-space undersampling, and PET activity reduction --- and inherits its statistical machinery from the test-of-equivalence procedures that the clinical-trial community has used for forty years but that medical-imaging methods papers have not adopted as a reporting standard.
\par
We provide a paired-bootstrap estimator with finite-sample coverage guarantees, a closed-form sample-size formula, and an open-source, pip-installable Python library (\texttt{pwm\_dose\_equivalence}) that produces credentials from any reconstruction method's image outputs. We exercise the framework end-to-end on synthetic data spanning CT, MRI, and PET through the same single API call, and specify per-modality real-cohort worked examples whose result tables populate at the Phase 1 and Phase 3 data milestones. To prevent silent benchmark drift, each credential is content-addressed by the SHA-256 hash of the framework definition it was issued against, so a reader given only a credential JSON can recover the exact framework version and recompute the verdict bit-for-bit.
\par
Adopted as a reporting standard, signal-equivalence converts marketing claims into reviewable scientific propositions that can be verified independently against the published evidence.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A central question in modern medical imaging is whether a new reconstruction method delivers diagnostically equivalent images at reduced patient exposure or scan time. The question takes a specific form in each modality: in computed tomography it is asked of reduced tube current (``low-dose CT''); in magnetic resonance imaging of $k$-space undersampling (``accelerated MRI''); in positron emission tomography of reduced injected radiotracer activity (``low-activity PET''). Across modalities, the dominant evaluation protocol is to report a single scalar metric --- peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), structural similarity (SSIM), root-mean-squared error (RMSE) --- alongside an unqualified claim such as ``$50\%$ dose reduction'' or ``$4\times$ acceleration.''

This reporting practice has three interconnected failures.

\paragraph{Failure 1: undefined task.} A reconstruction that preserves PSNR may smooth out the diagnostic feature it was supposed to preserve~\citep{kim2019smoothing,christianson2015}. Without specifying the downstream clinical task --- lung-nodule detection, lesion segmentation, organ quantification --- the equivalence claim is unanchored. A method may be ``dose-equivalent'' for lung-nodule detection while being ``dose-incompatible'' for ground-glass opacity characterization on the same scan.

\paragraph{Failure 2: undefined subpopulation.} Methods optimized on adult chest scans frequently degrade on pediatric or oncology-followup populations because the noise statistics, anatomical priors, and lesion-prevalence distributions differ~\citep{fda_aiml_2024,larson2018pediatric}. An equivalence claim that does not specify the patient distribution over which it holds cannot be transferred to a new clinical context without re-validation.

\paragraph{Failure 3: undefined statistics.} ``Equivalent'' as a binary verdict, without a margin $\varepsilon$ and a confidence level $\alpha$, is not testable. Test-of-equivalence statistics --- two one-sided tests (TOST), non-inferiority trial design --- have been the standard in pharmacokinetics and clinical-trial design for forty years~\citep{schuirmann1987tost,piaggio2012ni}, but have not been adopted as a methodological standard in medical-imaging methods papers.

We argue these three failures share a single solution: every equivalence claim should be a structured, probabilistic, task-conditional, subpopulation-conditional $5$-tuple credential. We call this the \emph{signal-equivalence framework}. The framework is intentionally modality-general: rather than producing one definition for CT (with parameter ``mA ratio''), a second for MRI (``acceleration factor''), and a third for PET (``activity ratio''), we adopt a single signal-reduction parameter $r \in (0,1]$ and a modality-specific signal-reduction operator $T_r$. The framework is then identical across modalities, while each modality's instantiation of $T_r$ encodes its physics.

\paragraph{Relation to existing equivalence statistics.} The statistical machinery is not new: test-of-equivalence procedures such as Schuirmann's two one-sided tests (TOST)~\citep{schuirmann1987tost} and non-inferiority trial design~\citep{piaggio2012ni} have served bioequivalence and clinical-trial endpoint reporting for nearly forty years, and CONSORT~\citep{schulz2010consort} requires that every randomized-trial publication report a margin and a confidence level. Medical-imaging \textit{methods} papers have not adopted this discipline. The signal-equivalence framework can be read as TOST applied to a paired-on-patient performance-difference estimator, with the four extra components --- explicit task $T$, explicit subpopulation $\Pi$, explicit signal-reduction operator $T_r$, and a content-addressed credential schema --- that are needed for the medical-imaging-methods setting but absent from the pharmacokinetic source material.

\paragraph{Relation to task-based image-quality assessment.} A separate, medical-imaging-internal lineage of work has long argued that image quality should be measured against a clinical task, not against scalar fidelity metrics --- the canonical statement is \citet{barrett1990objective}, and the textbook reference is \citet{barrett2013foundations}, which formalizes the Hotelling and channelized-Hotelling observer families and the ideal-observer figure-of-merit. AAPM Task Group 233~\citep{samei2019tg233} translates this lineage into a CT-specific performance-evaluation standard built around model observers on standardized phantoms. Signal-equivalence is \emph{complementary to}, not a replacement for, task-based image-quality assessment (TB-IQ). TB-IQ scores a single reconstruction-system configuration on a fixed phantom or test set, using a model observer as the figure of merit; it answers ``how good is system $S$?'' Signal-equivalence tests a pairwise equivalence claim between two methods at an explicit signal-reduction ratio over a named patient subpopulation; it answers ``does method $M$ at reduced signal match reference method $\Mref$ at full signal, by how much, and with what confidence?'' The two are usefully composed: any TB-IQ figure of merit that produces a real-valued score per case --- including the channelized-Hotelling observer of \citet{barrett2013foundations} --- is a valid choice for $P(T(\cdot), g(\cdot))$ inside a signal-equivalence credential. What signal-equivalence adds on top of the TB-IQ lineage is (a) explicit equivalence-margin and confidence-level reporting, (b) explicit subpopulation as a measure, (c) a signal-reduction operator $T_r$ that ties the claim to a specific physical reduction, and (d) a content-addressed credential format. None of these is present in the TB-IQ standard, and none of the TB-IQ contributions is duplicated here.

We make four separable contributions, each independently testable and adoptable.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A modality-general formalization of equivalence reporting for image-based clinical tasks.} The framework's mathematical kernel is a paired-on-patient equivalence test; what is novel is the type-signature discipline that ties an equivalence claim to a specific signal-reduction operator $T_r$ defined per modality (Section~\ref{sec:framework}), a task with explicit ground-truth protocol, a subpopulation expressed as a measure, and either an aggregate or per-patient formulation. The same definition covers CT tube-current reduction, MRI $k$-space undersampling, and PET activity reduction --- a unification not available in any existing reporting standard or statistics package.

    \item \textbf{An estimator with finite-sample guarantees and pre-registered sample-size targets} (Section~\ref{sec:methods-estimator}). The paired bootstrap is conservative for AUC near its boundary; we provide a bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) variant and an exact DeLong test variant for the AUC case, both wired into the same API, with automatic regime detection that warns when the percentile CI is anti-conservative.

    \item \textbf{An open-source, pip-installable, type-annotated, CI-tested Python library} (\texttt{pwm\_dose\_equivalence}; Apache 2.0) with worked-example notebooks for three modalities (Section~\ref{sec:methods-library}, Section~\ref{sec:software_rigor}). Reviewers can install the library, run the notebooks, and reproduce every result in this paper.

    \item \textbf{A content-addressed credential schema} that ties each published credential to the SHA-256 hash of the exact framework definition it was issued against (Section~\ref{sec:anchoring}). A reader holding only a credential JSON can recover the precise framework version, re-run the same library on the same test set, and verify the verdict bit-for-bit --- eliminating a class of benchmark-drift artifacts that has undermined longitudinal comparison in medical imaging~\citep{wang2020deeplearningreview}. Content-addressing is the methodological substance of the contribution; the choice of where to host the hash (a local file or a package registry) is a deployment detail discussed in Section~\ref{sec:anchoring}.
\end{enumerate}

A reader can adopt contribution 1 (the framework) without 4 (the registry); contribution 2 (the estimator) without 3 (the library); contribution 3 (the library) without 1 (by writing a different formal definition that the library compiles to). The four contributions are deliberately layered so that each subsequent one strengthens but does not depend on the previous one.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{Framework overview.} We define an \emph{imaging modality} as a triple $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{Y})$ consisting of a patient space $\mathcal{X}$, an acquisition space $\mathcal{S}$, and a reconstruction space $\mathcal{Y}$, together with a reference acquisition operator $\Sref: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})$ (probabilistic; acquisition is noisy). A \emph{signal-reduction operator at level} $r \in (0,1]$ is a map $T_r: \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})$ with the interpretation that $T_r(\Sref(x))$ is the raw measurement distribution one would obtain at reference-level acquisition scaled by $r$ (Section~\ref{sec:framework} formalizes this; the modality-specific instantiations are in Table~\ref{tab:modality_Tr}).

The signal-equivalence credential for a reconstruction method $M$ against a reference method $\Mref$ at the level $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha, \Pi)$ asserts that $M$'s expected task performance on reduced-signal scans is within $\varepsilon$ of $\Mref$'s expected task performance on full-signal scans, with confidence $\geq 1-\alpha$. The credential is computed via a paired bootstrap over patient draws from $\Pi$ (Section~\ref{sec:methods-estimator}); the verdict is one of \texttt{PASS}, \texttt{FAIL}, or \texttt{INDETERMINATE} (CI straddles the equivalence band).

Performance metric: lung-nodule detection AUC at fixed FPR = 0.1, with ground truth from majority-vote radiologist annotation. We built \texttt{pwm\_dose\_equivalence} rather than extending an existing tool for two reasons. \section{Discussion}

The signal-equivalence framework converts vendor marketing claims into reviewable scientific propositions. A claim of the form ``$50\%$ dose reduction'' is unfalsifiable; a claim of the form ``signal-equivalent at $(r=0.5, T=\textrm{lung-nodule-AUC}, \varepsilon=0.05, \alpha=0.05, \Pi=\textrm{adult-chest-LIDC})$ against $\Mref = \textrm{FBP-at-full-dose}$'' is falsifiable: re-run the paired-bootstrap on the same $\Pi$ and the same $T$, and the CI either passes or fails the equivalence band.

Three implications follow.

\paragraph{Reviewer practice.} A methods paper that does not report a $5$-tuple credential, or that reports one with $\varepsilon$ or $\alpha$ left unspecified, is not making a comparable claim. Journals adopting the framework as a reporting standard --- analogous to CONSORT for randomized controlled trials~\citep{schulz2010consort} --- would force authors to make claims that subsequent researchers can verify.

\paragraph{Regulatory practice.} The FDA's draft guidance on AI/ML-based software as a medical device~\citep{fda_aiml_2024} requires subgroup-stratified performance reporting; a $5$-tuple credential per subgroup operationalizes that requirement. A method certified at $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha, \Pi_{\textrm{adult}})$ but not at $\Pi_{\textrm{pediatric}}$ has explicit evidence of its subpopulation boundary, which is what regulatory reviewers ask for and rarely receive in current submissions. Because each credential is paired with a content-addressed framework hash, it also satisfies the FDA's emerging predetermined-change-control-plan (PCCP) expectations~\citep{fda_aiml_2024} for clear delineation of which performance claims are tied to which model version: a credential issued under \texttt{framework\_hash = sha256:abc...} cannot be silently re-evaluated against a later framework version without producing a new credential under the new hash.

\paragraph{Machine-readable verification.} Because a 5-tuple credential is structured and content-addressed, any third party --- a human reviewer, a CI script, or an automated evidence-aggregation agent~\citep{anthropic_mcp_2024} --- can recompute the verdict from the same test set under the same framework hash and obtain independent corroboration, rather than a re-reading of the abstract. A free-text claim such as ``equivalent at 50\% dose'' supports no such check.

\paragraph{Adoption pathway.} Reporting standards in medical imaging typically diffuse in three stages: a methods paper defines the standard; one or two journals require it for a specific subfield (as CONSORT was first adopted journal-by-journal before spreading); and a regulator eventually cites it. Signal-equivalence is positioned for that path --- the open-source library and the PWM Low-Dose CT Challenge~\citep{ws4leaderboard} provide an initial worked-example community, and the subgroup-stratified credential aligns with the FDA's AI/ML SaMD reporting expectations~\citep{fda_aiml_2024}. Any such trajectory is contingent on the framework's empirical performance and the library's maturation, which we will report honestly through the recurring challenge review.

\subsubsection*{Failure modes of the framework}
\label{sec:failure_modes}

The framework can return an actionable \texttt{PASS} or \texttt{FAIL} only when the data and the task are well-specified. We list the failure modes explicitly so users do not over-interpret a verdict in regimes where the framework is silent.

\paragraph{INDETERMINATE verdicts.} When the bootstrap CI straddles the equivalence band $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, the verdict is \texttt{INDETERMINATE}: the data are consistent with both equivalence and non-equivalence at the chosen $\varepsilon$ and $\alpha$. We refuse to force a binary verdict in this regime; honest reporting of \texttt{INDETERMINATE} is part of the framework, not a defect. \texttt{INDETERMINATE} typically indicates one of: (i) insufficient $n$ for the chosen $(\varepsilon, \alpha)$ --- mitigation: increase $n$ per the sample-size formula; (ii) high per-patient performance variance relative to the margin --- mitigation: tighten the subpopulation $\Pi$ to reduce variance, or relax $\varepsilon$; (iii) genuinely close-call performance where the method is neither demonstrably equivalent nor demonstrably worse.

\paragraph{Point-evaluated by design.} Signal-equivalence is a \emph{point-evaluated} framework: each credential is a separately verifiable claim at a specific $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha, \Pi)$, and algebraic combinations of credentials are deliberately not derivable from one another. This is a design choice, not an omission. Combining credentials algebraically --- across signal ratios, tasks, subpopulations, or modalities --- introduces unsound chaining risks that have repeatedly produced inflated claims in adjacent fields~\citep{wang2020deeplearningreview}; we prefer the conservative position that any new operating point requires its own credential. Four specific claim types are therefore out of scope, by design rather than by oversight:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Superiority} (``$M$ is better than $\Mref$ at $r$''). Signal-equivalence is a two-sided equivalence test; superiority is a one-sided test of a different null hypothesis. Both are appropriate procedures, but they answer different scientific questions and the framework keeps the two reports distinct.
    \item \textit{Cross-modality transfer} (``$M$ trained on CT works on MRI''). Each modality has its own reference acquisition operator $\Sref$ and signal-reduction family $\{T_r\}$, so a credential under one modality is mathematically silent about the other. Cross-modality claims are admitted only as a new credential under the target modality's $T_r$.
    \item \textit{Composition} (``If $M \equiv \Mref$ at $r$ and $\Mref \equiv \Mref'$ at $r'$, then $M \equiv \Mref'$ at $r r'$''). The framework is intentionally not transitive across reference choices; users observing two credentials at adjacent operating points may report both, but a composed claim requires a new bootstrap at the composed operating point.
    \item \textit{Multi-task simultaneity} (``$M$ is equivalent for tasks $T_1$ \emph{and} $T_2$''). A v1 credential is task-specific, which keeps the equivalence margin $\varepsilon$ interpretable on a single metric scale; a future task-bundle extension is described in Supplementary~\todo{supp v2}.
\end{itemize}

A user wanting a composite claim is asked to issue and publish the corresponding component credentials --- which is itself the reporting standard the framework is trying to establish. The case against a derivable composition law (in particular, why the intuitive $r'' = r r'$, $\varepsilon'' = \varepsilon + \varepsilon'$, $\alpha'' = \alpha + \alpha'$ guess fails) is worked out in [\texttt{theory/proofs/composition.md}].

\paragraph{Estimator failure modes.} The paired bootstrap is consistent for finite-second-moment performance differences, but: (i) AUC near 1.0 with small $n$ can produce mildly non-nominal percentile-bootstrap coverage --- the library defaults to the closed-form DeLong CI for AUC tasks (mildly conservative; $\sim 300\times$ faster than the bootstrap variants per the coverage simulation in [\texttt{experiments/estimator\_coverage/}]) and reports a Monte-Carlo standard error in all cases so users can detect borderline regimes; (ii) heavy-tailed metric distributions can require larger $B$ than the default 10{,}000 --- the same Monte-Carlo SE diagnostic surfaces this; (iii) ground-truth label noise that exceeds $\varepsilon$ makes any equivalence verdict meaningless --- the library refuses to issue a credential when the documented adjudication-protocol disagreement rate exceeds $\varepsilon / 2$.

\paragraph{Subpopulation specificity.} A credential under subpopulation $\Pi$ is a claim about $\Pi$ specifically and carries no algebraic implication for $\Pi' \neq \Pi$. This is the same point-evaluated discipline as above: where users would like a cross-subpopulation guarantee, the framework asks them to compute and report a separate per-subpopulation credential. The library accordingly does not produce ``aggregate'' credentials across subpopulations, and the FDA's subgroup-stratified reporting expectation~\citep{fda_aiml_2024} aligns naturally with this convention.

\paragraph{Aggregate-vs-per-patient.} The aggregate formulation (Definition~\ref{def:signal_equiv_aggregate}) matches clinical-trial endpoint reporting but is silent about per-patient regret. For settings where the cost of a single missed lesion dominates (rare disease, oncology screening, pediatric), the per-patient strengthening (Definition~\ref{def:signal_equiv_per_patient}) should be opted into. The library exposes both formulations through a single \texttt{formulation=} argument; the default is \texttt{aggregate}.

\paragraph{Reproducibility caveats.} A published credential is reproducible only if the test set, the method, the reference method, the bootstrap seed, and the framework version are all reproducible. Content-addressing (Section~\ref{sec:anchoring}) covers the framework definition by its SHA-256 hash, and the test-set manifest by a separate dataset-spec hash; the method's \texttt{code\_hash} field covers the method. The library refuses to compute a credential when the method or reference method has not been wrapped in a hash-addressable artifact (Docker image with SHA-256 digest, or pinned PyPI release).

\subsubsection*{Limitations of the present work}
\label{sec:limitations}

The framework above is methodologically complete --- in the sense that the credential schema, the estimator, the sample-size formulas, and the audit surface together form a self-contained reporting standard --- but the present manuscript ships with a deliberately scoped empirical-validation surface. We list the limitations explicitly so a reviewer and a downstream adopter can calibrate what we are and are not claiming at this stage. Each limitation paragraph below has a corresponding entry in \texttt{paper\_draft/limitations\_anchors.md} (companion to \texttt{paper\_draft/reproduction\_guide.md}) mapping the limitation to the proofs / simulation document that backs it and to the roadmap milestone that would close it.

\paragraph{Empirical validation is synthetic-anchored, not yet real-cohort.} The current evidence backing the modality-general claim is the synthetic cross-modality consistency table (Results, Table~\ref{tab:consistency}; 6~credentials at seed = 42 covering CT, MRI, and PET) and the per-modality estimator-coverage simulations (Methods Section~\ref{sec:estimator}; 69 coverage cells across AUC, Dice, and contrast-recovery --- 27 / 18 / 24 respectively --- plus a 24-cell power simulation, 93 simulated $(n, \sigma, \rho)$ cells in total). The per-modality Results tables (Table~\ref{tab:ct_results}, Table~\ref{tab:mri_results}, Table~\ref{tab:pet_results}) carry \todo{} placeholders that fill at Phase 1 (CT, target D9 + 90 from the PWM-LDCT 1.0 cohort), Phase 3a (MRI, target D9 + 270 via fastMRI), and Phase 3b (PET, target D9 + 270 via the NEMA NU-2 IQ phantom). A reviewer reading the present manuscript should treat the cross-modality consistency demonstration as evidence that the same code path produces sensible verdicts across modalities, not as evidence that the framework correctly discriminates a real candidate method on a real cohort. The reproduction guide (\texttt{paper\_draft/reproduction\_guide.md}) is explicit about which numerical claims are synthetic-anchored versus real-cohort.

\paragraph{Three modalities are validated; the extension surface is not.} Methods Table~\ref{tab:modality_dispatch} specifies the canonical $T_r$ operator for CT (Poisson thinning of projection counts), MRI (variable-density Cartesian mask, with \texttt{mask\_family} carried inside $\Pi$), and PET (activity reduction); additional modalities --- Optical / fluorescence, OCT, ultrasound --- are listed as ``user-implementable, not validated.'' The R3-3 tutorial notebook (\texttt{notebooks/04\_optical\_extending.py}) demonstrates that the API accepts an arbitrary modality string, but no independent research group has yet computed a credential under a user-implemented modality, and the modality-specific operator validity for those extensions remains the user's responsibility. We expect external adoption (Months 0--12 of the adoption pathway) to surface the first independent extensions; we do not claim the framework is correct for arbitrary modalities at the present manuscript stage.

\paragraph{The credential schema does not yet pin the method bundle.} The v0.2 schema carries \texttt{method} and \texttt{reference\_method} as bare-string slugs; the manuscript's `code\_hash` discipline (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) currently lives \emph{at the publication venue} (a Docker image digest in the supplementary materials, a pinned PyPI release hash, etc.) rather than inside the credential JSON itself. The companion credential reading guide (\texttt{paper\_draft/credential\_reading\_guide.md}) and the library's CONTRIBUTING.md document this as the planned widening at v1.0 (Tier-A schema bump to \texttt{\{name, code\_hash\}}); until then, method-bundle provenance is recorded out-of-band and is not enforced by the audit surface. A reviewer evaluating a credential should ask the authors for the matching method-bundle hash and confirm it independently.

\paragraph{Reader-variability is a hard ceiling on attainable $\varepsilon$.} The framework's verdicts are conditional on ground-truth label quality; in the AUC / Dice regime the label-noise floor is whatever the reader-panel adjudication protocol leaves on the table. Concretely: a credential at $\varepsilon = 0.02$ on a task whose inter-reader Dice variance has standard deviation $\sim 0.05$ is operating below the label-noise floor and the verdict is largely a function of which adjudication subsample fell into the test split, not of the candidate method's actual signal-equivalence. The library refuses to issue a credential when the documented adjudication-protocol disagreement rate exceeds $\varepsilon / 2$ (Section~\ref{sec:failure_modes}, ``Estimator failure modes''); however, on tasks where reader-panel disagreement has not been independently quantified, the user is responsible for choosing $\varepsilon$ above the plausible label-noise floor. We do not currently provide a per-task label-noise registry; building one is on the v1.0 roadmap.

\paragraph{The headline WS-1 1.0 cohort sits in the INDETERMINATE-dominated regime for the recommended AUC default.} At the recommended AUC-task default $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and a typical lung-nodule operating point AUC $\approx 0.92$, the 1.0 cohort of 208 unique paired patients satisfies the (S3) sample-size formula (n $\approx$ 130--250 required) but the empirical verdict distribution is dominated by \texttt{INDETERMINATE} rather than \texttt{PASS}: P(\texttt{PASS}) under the null is approximately $0.40$ at $n = 200$, lifting to approximately $0.94$ at $n = 500$ (Section~\ref{sec:estimator}, ``Cohort-sizing implication''). Downstream users issuing credentials on this cohort should expect \texttt{INDETERMINATE} verdicts for many genuinely equivalent methods; absence of \texttt{PASS} on the 1.0 cohort is not evidence of non-equivalence, only of insufficient $n$ at this $(\varepsilon, \alpha)$. For Dice / MAE / contrast-recovery tasks at the recommended non-AUC default $\varepsilon = 0.02$, the same cohort is comfortably in the PASS regime per the per-modality estimator-coverage simulations. The prospective companion cohort target (n $\geq 500$) lifts the AUC operating point into the reliable-PASS regime. This is a property of the dataset $\times$ operating-point combination, not of the framework itself; we surface it as a limitation so that readers do not misread \texttt{INDETERMINATE} verdicts on the 1.0 cohort as negative results.

\subsection{The framework, formally}
\label{sec:framework}

An imaging modality is a triple $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{Y})$ of measurable spaces (patient space, acquisition space, reconstruction space) together with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A \emph{reference acquisition operator} $\Sref: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})$. For each patient $x \in \mathcal{X}$, $\Sref(x)$ is the distribution over raw measurements that the full-signal scanner produces on $x$ (probabilistic because acquisition is noisy).
    \item A family of \emph{signal-reduction operators} $\{T_r\}_{r \in (0,1]}$ with $T_r: \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})$. The interpretation: if $s \sim \Sref(x)$, then $s' \sim T_r(\Sref(x))$ is the raw measurement at reference-level scaled by $r$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What ``modality-general'' means, precisely.} The cross-modality claim has both a mathematical and an empirical component, and we are careful to distinguish them.

\textit{Mathematically}, the credential definition (Definition~\ref{def:signal_equiv_aggregate}) is parametric in $(\mathcal{M}, T_r, T, P)$. Any imaging modality with a well-defined reference acquisition operator $\Sref$ and a measurable family of signal-reduction operators $\{T_r\}_{r \in (0,1]}$ admits a credential under this definition. The paired-bootstrap estimator (Algorithm~\ref{alg:paired_bootstrap}) is similarly parametric: it requires only that $P(T(\cdot), g(\cdot))$ produce a real-valued score per case. Both pieces are modality-agnostic by construction; no modality-specific code is required to compile a new modality.

\textit{Empirically}, the three modalities we ship as validated have been exercised end-to-end through the synthetic cross-modality consistency demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:results}, Table~\ref{tab:consistency}) and the per-modality estimator-coverage simulations (Section~\ref{sec:methods-estimator}); real-cohort checks against published baselines are scheduled at the Phase 1 (CT) and Phase 3 (MRI, PET) data milestones (Data availability). \textit{What would falsify the modality-general claim.} If the framework's verdicts on MRI or PET disagreed systematically with established clinical-equivalence assessments at the same operating point, the modality-general claim would fail. We do not observe such disagreement in the synthetic cross-modality consistency demonstration reported here (Section~\ref{sec:results}); the real-cohort check becomes available when the per-modality result tables populate at the Phase 1 and Phase 3 data milestones. We call out the falsifiability explicitly because reviewer scrutiny of cross-modality claims is appropriate: any single-modality TOST is uncontroversial, but uniform applicability across physical-acquisition models is the contribution that needs evidence.

A \emph{reconstruction method} is a measurable map $M: \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{Y}$. The composed map $M \circ T_r \circ \Sref: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})$ is ``reconstruct from a reduced-signal scan of patient $x$.'' We compare two such composed maps: $\Mred := M \circ T_r \circ \Sref$ (the method under evaluation, on reduced-signal data) and $\mathcal{M}_{\textrm{base}} \circ \Sref$ (a named reference method on full-signal data; we write $\Mref$ for shorthand).

A \emph{task} is a measurable map $T: \mathcal{Y} \to \mathcal{D}$ from reconstructions to a clinical decision space $\mathcal{D}$ (binary detection, bounding boxes, per-voxel segmentation masks, scalar quantification, etc.). Ground truth $g(x) \in \mathcal{D}$ is established by a documented adjudication protocol --- a board-certified reader panel with a calibration gate and consolidation rule for image-based tasks, or a physical reference standard for phantom tasks --- and the protocol identifier is part of the credential. The operational details, including the specific reader-panel discipline used for the CT worked example, are given in Section~\ref{sec:methods-estimator}.

A \emph{performance metric} is a functional $P: \mathcal{D} \times \mathcal{D} \to \R$ evaluating ``how well the task output on a reconstruction agrees with ground truth.''

A \emph{subpopulation} is a probability measure $\Pi$ on $\mathcal{X}$ together with an operational description (recruitment / inclusion criteria a trial coordinator could use to verify draws from $\Pi$). Crucially, $\Pi$ also carries the \emph{acquisition-protocol metadata} that fully determines $T_r$ for a member of $\mathcal{M}$ --- for CT, the scanner vendor, kVp, reconstruction kernel, and any vendor-specific dose-modulation profile; for MRI, the $k$-space mask family (uniform random, variable-density Cartesian, equispaced + central-fully-sampled) and the coil sensitivities; for PET, the tracer, scanner, and whether reduction is by injected activity or scan-time. Two subpopulations with the same demographic description but different acquisition protocols are different $\Pi$ and yield distinct credentials. This convention is what allows the 5-tuple to stay 5-tuple across modalities: modality-specific operational parameters that change the meaning of ``signal reduction'' (most notably the MRI mask family) are recorded as fields of $\Pi$, not as a new tuple slot. The MRI-mask-family case is worked out separately in [\texttt{theory/proofs/mri\_mask.md}].

\begin{definition}[Signal-equivalence, aggregate formulation]
\label{def:signal_equiv_aggregate}
A reconstruction method $M$ is \emph{signal-equivalent} to a reference method $\Mref$ at level $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha)$ over subpopulation $\Pi$, written $M \equiv_{(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha, \Pi)} \Mref$, iff
\begin{equation}
    \left| \E_{x \sim \Pi}\left[ \E_{y \sim \Mred(x)} P(T(y), g(x)) \right] - \E_{x \sim \Pi}\left[ \E_{y' \sim \Mref(x)} P(T(y'), g(x)) \right] \right| < \varepsilon \label{eq:signal_equiv_main}
\end{equation}
with confidence $\geq 1 - \alpha$ under the paired-bootstrap estimator of Section~\ref{sec:methods-estimator}.
\end{definition}

Definition~\ref{def:signal_equiv_aggregate} reads: \textit{``the population-mean task performance of $M$ on reduced-signal scans is within $\varepsilon$ of the population-mean task performance of $\Mref$ on full-signal scans, with confidence $1-\alpha$.''}

\paragraph{Population claim vs.\ sample evidence.} The two expectations in Equation~\eqref{eq:signal_equiv_main} are deterministic population objects that the credential \emph{asserts}; the role of $\alpha$ and the paired-bootstrap procedure of Section~\ref{sec:methods-estimator} is to deliver finite-sample \emph{evidence} for or against that assertion from a test set $\{x_k\}_{k=1}^n$ drawn from $\Pi$. The credential schema therefore records, alongside $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha, \Pi)$, the test-set size $n$ and a slug resolving to the operational sampling protocol by which draws from $\Pi$ are produced (e.g.\ the dataset's published recruitment criteria and split manifest), so that an independent verifier is guaranteed to be testing the same population claim --- not merely recomputing the same bootstrap CI on a possibly-different sample.

We also define a strictly-stronger \emph{per-patient} formulation:

\begin{definition}[Signal-equivalence, per-patient formulation]
\label{def:signal_equiv_per_patient}
A method $M$ is \emph{per-patient signal-equivalent} to $\Mref$ at level $(r, T, \varepsilon, \alpha)$ over $\Pi$ iff
\begin{equation}
    \Prob_{x \sim \Pi}\!\left[ \left| \E_{y \sim \Mred(x)} P(T(y), g(x)) - \E_{y' \sim \Mref(x)} P(T(y'), g(x)) \right| < \varepsilon \right] \geq 1 - \alpha.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

We adopt Definition~\ref{def:signal_equiv_aggregate} as canonical because it matches clinical-trial endpoint reporting. Definition~\ref{def:signal_equiv_per_patient} is an opt-in strengthening (e.g., for pediatric or rare-disease subpopulations where per-patient regret dominates).

\subsection{Type signatures: six clarifications}
\label{sec:framework_clarifications}

A naive informal version of Equation~\eqref{eq:signal_equiv_main} conflates six distinctions that must be separated before any theorem can be cleanly stated. We list them here because reviewers may otherwise read the framework as a re-statement of TOST.

\textbf{(C1) Patient vs image.} $T_r$ acts on the \emph{acquisition} of a patient, not on the patient. The patient $x \in \mathcal{X}$ is latent; the framework never observes $x$ directly.

\textbf{(C2) $T_r$ is an operator, not a scalar multiplier.} Writing ``$\Sred = r \cdot \Sref$'' is dimensionally wrong: $\Sred$ is an operator-valued map, parameterized by $r$.

\textbf{(C3) Reference acquisition $\neq$ reference algorithm.} ``Reference'' is ambiguous in the informal version. We separate $\Sref$ (the operator, full-signal scan) from $\Mref$ (the reconstruction method).

\textbf{(C4) Performance is a function of $(T(y), g(x))$, not $(M, S, T)$.} The metric evaluates the task output against ground truth; the method, scan, and task generate the output but are not its arguments.

\textbf{(C5) Subpopulation is a measure that carries acquisition-protocol metadata, not just a demographic label.} Two subpopulations with the same demographic label but different operational definitions --- or the same demographic but different scanner / mask / tracer settings --- are different subpopulations. This is what lets modality-specific acquisition parameters (CT vendor, MRI mask family, PET reduction mode) live inside $\Pi$ rather than as extra tuple slots, preserving a single 5-tuple shape across CT, MRI, and PET.

\textbf{(C6) Aggregate vs per-patient.} Definitions~\ref{def:signal_equiv_aggregate} and~\ref{def:signal_equiv_per_patient} are not equivalent; per-patient implies aggregate, but not conversely.

\subsection{Estimator}
\label{sec:methods-estimator}

\paragraph{Ground-truth protocol.} Ground truth $g(x_k)$ is the largest single source of credential variance the framework does not control, so the credential records the adjudication protocol by content-addressed reference rather than by free-text slug. For the CT worked example, whose annotation run is scheduled at the Phase 1 data milestone, we specify the PWM-LDCT 1.0 annotation QA protocol~\citep{ws1dataset} verbatim, summarized here for the reader's convenience: (i) every annotator is a board-certified radiologist with $\geq 5$ years of thoracic CT experience; (ii) each annotator passes a $20$-case calibration set drawn from a held-out LIDC-IDRI subset before contributing release annotations, with a pre-registered Cohen's $\kappa \geq 0.60$ pass threshold against the LIDC majority vote; (iii) candidate nodules are matched across readers by bounding-box $\mathrm{IoU} \geq 0.3$ on the same slice with $\pm 1$-slice 3D adjacency; (iv) a candidate is included in $g(x_k)$ iff more than half of the qualified readers for the case marked it, with attribute consolidation via element-wise median (\texttt{bbox}, \texttt{diameter\_mm}, ordinal \texttt{texture}) and mode (\texttt{location}); (v) two-reader cases with non-overlapping boxes, $\geq 1.5\times$ diameter disagreement, or texture-class disagreement are referred to a third independent radiologist whose label becomes the released ground truth; (vi) drift is monitored by a rotating 5-case re-calibration every $\approx 50$ cases, and any reader whose running $\kappa$ falls below the pass threshold is paused and all of their since-last-passing-window cases are re-annotated. The per-reader raw labels are preserved alongside the consolidated ground truth so users of the framework can substitute alternative consolidation rules (e.g.\ STAPLE) and re-issue the credential without re-running the reader panel. The MRI worked example reuses the published fastMRI expert meniscus segmentations as ground truth, with the segmentation protocol resolved through the same content-addressed mechanism; the PET worked example does not require a reader panel because the NEMA NU-2 IQ phantom provides physical ground truth (known sphere sizes and activity concentrations). In all three cases the credential's \texttt{ground\_truth\_protocol} field resolves to a normative protocol document by SHA-256 hash (Section~\ref{sec:anchoring}), so a downstream verifier can confirm the protocol bit-for-bit rather than from a slug whose meaning may drift.

\paragraph{Label-noise refusal threshold.} The library refuses to issue a credential when the documented inter-reader disagreement rate of the cited protocol exceeds $\varepsilon / 2$; below that threshold, equivalence verdicts cannot be meaningfully separated from annotation noise. For the CT protocol above, the published pairwise $\kappa$ on the calibration set is the operational input to this check.

Given a test set $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$ drawn i.i.d.\ from $\Pi$ with ground truth $\{g(x_k)\}$ established by the protocol above, and methods $M, \Mref$, we estimate Equation~\eqref{eq:signal_equiv_main} via a paired bootstrap on patients (Algorithm~\ref{alg:paired_bootstrap}).

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
    \caption{Paired-bootstrap signal-equivalence credential}
    \label{alg:paired_bootstrap}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require Test set $\{x_k\}_{k=1}^n$ from $\Pi$; ground truth $\{g(x_k)\}$; methods $M, \Mref$; reduction operator $T_r$; task $T$; metric $P$; margin $\varepsilon$; significance $\alpha$; replicates $B$; seed.
        \For{$k = 1, \ldots, n$}
            \State $s_{\textrm{red}} \sim T_r(\Sref(x_k))$;\quad $a_k \gets P(T(M(s_{\textrm{red}})),\, g(x_k))$
            \State $s_{\textrm{ref}} \sim \Sref(x_k)$;\quad $b_k \gets P(T(\Mref(s_{\textrm{ref}})),\, g(x_k))$
        \EndFor
        \For{$b = 1, \ldots, B$}
            \State Sample $n$ indices $\{i_1, \ldots, i_n\}$ with replacement from $\{1, \ldots, n\}$
            \State $\Delta_b \gets \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n (a_{i_j} - b_{i_j})$
        \EndFor
        \State $[\ell, u] \gets$ central $(1-\alpha)$ percentile interval of $\{\Delta_b\}$
        \State \Return verdict $\gets$ \textbf{if}~$[\ell, u] \subset (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$~\textbf{then}~\texttt{PASS}; \textbf{else if}~$[\ell, u] \cap (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) = \emptyset$~\textbf{then}~\texttt{FAIL}; \textbf{else}~\texttt{INDETERMINATE}
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We use $B = 10{,}000$ as the default. Sampling pairs (rather than the two arms independently) is essential because $a_k$ and $b_k$ are correlated through the patient $x_k$.

\paragraph{Sample-size formula.} For a target $(\varepsilon, \alpha)$ and a per-patient performance-difference standard deviation $\sigma_\Delta = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_k)}$, the leading-order normal-approximation bound for a sample-mean estimator is
\begin{equation}
    n \;\geq\; \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\!2} \sigma_\Delta^2.
    \label{eq:sample_size_general}
\end{equation}
With per-arm SD $\sigma$ and cross-arm correlation $\rho$, $\sigma_\Delta^2 = 2 \sigma^2 (1 - \rho)$, so pairing reduces the required $n$ by a factor $(1-\rho)/2^{-1}$ relative to two independent arms. For paired AUC, $\hat\Delta$ is not a sample-mean estimator; the appropriate specialisation uses the DeLong placement-difference standard deviation $s$,
\begin{equation}
    n_{\text{AUC}} \;\geq\; \left(\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\varepsilon}\right)^{\!2} \cdot 4 s^2.
    \label{eq:sample_size_auc}
\end{equation}
At $\rho = 0.5$ on a binormal model, empirical $s$ values are approximately $s \approx 0.20, 0.15, 0.08$ at AUC $\approx 0.85, 0.92, 0.97$ respectively (full table and derivation in [\texttt{theory/proofs/sample\_size.md}]). Substituting in~\eqref{eq:sample_size_auc} gives the **AUC-task default $(\varepsilon = 0.05, \alpha = 0.05)$**, which yields $n \approx 130$--$250$ across the typical operating range --- comfortably within the WS-1 PWM-LDCT cohort. For Dice, MAE, and per-patient contrast-recovery (where $\hat\Delta$ is a sample-mean estimator and~\eqref{eq:sample_size_general} applies directly), the canonical default is $\varepsilon = 0.02$; users with a clinical argument for a tighter or looser margin can specify it, and the library will require the corresponding $n$. A finite-sample Bernstein correction (strictly more conservative than~\eqref{eq:sample_size_general}) is documented in Supplementary Section~\ref{supp:bernstein} and in [\texttt{theory/proofs/sample\_size.md}] §4.

\paragraph{Estimator defaults.} The percentile bootstrap is the library's default for general metrics. For paired AUC, the closed-form DeLong test (DeLong, DeLong \& Clarke-Pearson 1988) is auto-selected: in 27-cell coverage simulations under the truly-equivalent null ($\Delta = 0$) at AUC $\in \{0.85, 0.92, 0.97\}$ and $n \in \{100, 200, 500\}$, DeLong is mildly conservative (median coverage $\approx 0.96$) and approximately $300\times$ faster than the bootstrap variants --- a strict improvement over percentile in the AUC case. The bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) variant remains available as an opt-in; under the null it does not robustly outperform percentile (in the same 27-cell sweep BCa coverage falls below the nominal $0.95$ at AUC $\geq 0.92$, $n = 100$), so we do not default to it. Full coverage table and the estimator-default justification are in [\texttt{theory/proofs/estimator.md}], backed by the seed-reproducible simulation in [\texttt{experiments/estimator\_coverage/}].

\label{sec:methods-library}

    cred = signal_equivalence_credential(

Strict types surface the error at the API boundary.

\subsection{Content-addressed credential schema}
\label{sec:anchoring}

The credential schema is content-addressed: every published credential carries the SHA-256 hash of the exact framework definition document it was issued against. A published credential identifies the framework version by hash:

\begin{verbatim}
    {
      "schema_version": "pwm-signal-equivalence/v0.2",
      "framework_hash": "sha256:...",
      "credential": { ... }
    }
\end{verbatim}

The hash uniquely identifies the framework definition the credential commits to. A reader verifies the credential by (i) retrieving the framework definition document by hash, (ii) re-running the paired bootstrap on the same test set, and (iii) checking that the recomputed verdict matches the published verdict. This eliminates a class of benchmark-drift artifacts in which a method's reported performance is silently re-evaluated against a different framework version after submission~\citep{wang2020deeplearningreview}.

\paragraph{Internal-consistency audit.} A reader who lacks the test-set scores (for example, a regulator without raw-DICOM access) can still check the credential's \emph{internal} consistency without re-running the bootstrap. The library exposes a one-call audit function, \texttt{pwm\_dose\_equivalence.audit\_credential}, which validates the credential JSON against the machine-readable schema (\texttt{CREDENTIAL\_JSON\_SCHEMA}), confirms the framework hash matches the installed library version, re-derives the verdict from the published $(\Delta_{\mathrm{CI},\mathrm{lo}}, \Delta_{\mathrm{CI},\mathrm{hi}}, \varepsilon)$ and checks that it matches the stored verdict, inspects the \texttt{sample\_size\_check} field for the undersized-PASS pattern, and flags BCa-as-headline credentials lacking a percentile companion. The audit returns a \texttt{CredentialAudit} dataclass distinguishing hard issues (block \texttt{ok}) from soft warnings (recorded but non-blocking). The same audit is available from the shell as the installed console entry point \texttt{pwm-audit} (with file / stdin / \texttt{--json} input modes and a 0-on-ok / 1-on-issue / 2-on-error exit-code contract), so reviewers, CI hooks, and submission-checklist scripts can verify a credential without writing any Python. Full bootstrap reproduction remains the job of the reproduction guide; the audit is the cheaper preflight check that catches tampered or schema-mismatched credentials before the bootstrap would.

\paragraph{Hash-resolution backends.} Content-addressing is the methodological substance; \emph{where} the hash is resolved is a deployment choice. The library accepts any of three resolvers: (a) a local file containing the framework definition (sufficient for self-contained reproduction during peer review); (b) a package-registry resolver that retrieves the definition by hash from the PyPI release used to issue the credential; or (c) an optional public registry mirror. Resolvers (a) and (b) are sufficient for the methodological guarantees stated above; resolver (c) is an operational convenience for community deployments and is not required for the framework to function.

\section{Bernstein finite-sample correction to the sample-size formula}
\label{supp:bernstein}

The leading-order sample-size bound (Equation~\eqref{eq:sample_size_general} in the main text) is an asymptotic-normal approximation. For credentials issued at small $n$ (say $n < 100$) the asymptotic regime is shaky and a finite-sample bound is appropriate. This section derives the Bernstein-type bound the library reports alongside (S1).

\subsection{Setup}

Let $\Delta_1, \ldots, \Delta_n$ be i.i.d.\ random variables with $\mathbb{E}[\Delta_k] = \mu_\Delta$, $\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_k) = \sigma_\Delta^2$, and $|\Delta_k - \mu_\Delta| \leq M$ almost surely. For task metrics bounded in $[0, 1]$ (AUC, Dice, normalised contrast-recovery), the per-patient difference $\Delta_k = a_k - b_k$ satisfies $|\Delta_k| \leq 1$, so $M = 1$ is a defensible default; for unbounded metrics (e.g.\ MAE on Hounsfield-unit images) $M$ must be specified by the user.

\subsection{Bernstein's inequality}

Bernstein's inequality (see, e.g., Boucheron, Lugosi \& Massart 2013, Theorem 2.10) states that for $t > 0$,
\begin{equation}
    \Pr\!\left[\,\left|\bar\Delta_n - \mu_\Delta\right| > t\,\right]
    \;\leq\; 2 \exp\!\left(\,-\frac{n t^2}{2 \sigma_\Delta^2 + \tfrac{2 M t}{3}}\,\right).
    \label{eq:bernstein}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Substitution at $t = \varepsilon$}

We require the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:bernstein} to be at most $\alpha$. Setting $t = \varepsilon$ and rearranging,
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{\;n_{\text{Bernstein}} \;\geq\; \log\!\left(\tfrac{2}{\alpha}\right) \cdot \frac{2\sigma_\Delta^2 + \tfrac{2 M \varepsilon}{3}}{\varepsilon^2}.\;}
    \label{eq:S4}
\end{equation}

This is~(S4) from the proofs document. The structure has two interpretable terms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Variance term} $2 \sigma_\Delta^2$: dominates when the per-patient differences are noisy relative to the margin. At the canonical $\alpha = 0.05$, $\log(2/\alpha) \approx 3.689$, so the variance-only Bernstein requirement is $\sim 3.689 \cdot 2 \sigma_\Delta^2 / \varepsilon^2 = 7.378 \sigma_\Delta^2 / \varepsilon^2$, which exceeds the CLT bound $z_{0.975}^2 \sigma_\Delta^2 / \varepsilon^2 \approx 3.842 \sigma_\Delta^2 / \varepsilon^2$ by a factor of $\approx 1.92$. This factor-of-two is the price Bernstein pays for being a finite-sample guarantee, not an asymptotic one.
    \item \textit{Bound term} $\tfrac{2 M \varepsilon}{3}$: dominates when the per-patient differences are nearly deterministic relative to the margin. At $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and $M = 1$, the term equals $0.0333$, which is comparable to $2\sigma_\Delta^2$ at $\sigma_\Delta \approx 0.13$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Numerical comparison to (S1)}

At the canonical AUC operating point $(\varepsilon = 0.05, \alpha = 0.05, M = 1, \sigma_\Delta = 0.10)$:

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{lr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Formula} & \textbf{$n_{\min}$} \\
        \midrule
        (S1) Asymptotic CLT (Eq.~\ref{eq:sample_size_general})         & $\geq 16$ \\
        (S4) Bernstein finite-sample (Eq.~\ref{eq:S4})                 & $\geq 50$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

At $(\varepsilon = 0.02, \sigma_\Delta = 0.10)$ the ratio widens further: (S1) gives $n \geq 97$ vs (S4) $n \geq 308$. The widening is consistent: as $\varepsilon$ shrinks, Bernstein's bound term contribution shrinks linearly while the variance term contribution remains fixed, and the leading prefactor of $\log(2/\alpha) / z_{1-\alpha/2}^2 \approx 0.96$ on the variance term combined with the factor of $2$ on $\sigma_\Delta^2$ gives the asymptotic ratio of $\approx 1.92$.

\subsection{Library behaviour and practical guidance}

The \texttt{pwm\_dose\_equivalence} library computes both~(S1) and~(S4) when a $\sigma_\Delta$ hint is supplied and reports both in the credential's \texttt{sample\_size\_check} field. The library emits a Bernstein-explicit note when the observed $n$ falls below~(S4) but above~(S1) --- this is the regime where the credential is asymptotically valid but does not have a finite-sample guarantee, and a reviewer may want to know which discipline the credential was issued under.

We recommend reporting the (S1) bound as the operational $n$-target for credentials at $n \geq 100$, and (S4) as the operational target for credentials at $n < 100$. The framework remains parametric in the user's choice: the canonical defaults are suggestions, not constraints.

\subsection{Caveats}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bernstein's inequality requires $|\Delta_k - \mu_\Delta| \leq M$ almost surely. For unbounded metrics this is not satisfied; in that case Hoeffding's inequality with sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential moment conditions applies, with the same general structure but a different bound term. Users specifying unbounded metrics should pass $M = \infty$ to the library, which then reports only the (S1) CLT bound.
    \item For paired AUC, $\bar\Delta_n$ is a U-statistic rather than a sample mean. Bernstein's inequality for U-statistics (see Hoeffding 1948; Arcones \& Giné 1993) gives a structurally similar bound with $M$ replaced by the maximum placement-difference; the factor of $4$ in (S3) carries through. The (S4) bound as stated is therefore loose for AUC; users with $n < 100$ and AUC tasks should use the DeLong closed-form CI (which has a finite-sample variance estimator) rather than rely on (S4).
\end{itemize}
